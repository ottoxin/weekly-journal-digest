- **Hold That Thought: Enhancing the Durability of Fact-Checking Effects Through Low-Cost Accuracy Reminders**
  Published: 2026-08-21
  Authors: Je Hoon Chae; Tim Groeling; Hyunjin (Jin) Song
  Affiliations: University of California; Yonsei University
  DOI: 10.1080/10584609.2026.2716761
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2716761
  Abstract: Prior evidence suggests that fact-checking can update beliefs regarding factually incorrect information in the short term but its effects often decay rapidly. Such decay raises normative concerns about its sustainability and real-world impact. Here, we examine whether a cognitively efficient, low-cost intervention – called an “accuracy reminder” – can enhance the durability of fact-checking effects. In a large-scale pre-registered panel longitudinal experiment (N = 7,188), participants were randomly assigned to receive fact-checks and, under some conditions, follow-up prompts to reevaluate the same claim at interval-optimized time points. Across topical domains and partisan identities, reminder conditions produced durable belief correction effects lasting up to four weeks, more than doubling the persistence observed without reminders. Effects were robust across reminder frequency and timing, as well as against individual differences, suggesting implementation flexibility and theoretical generalizability. These findings identify retrieval-based reinforcement as a scalable means of sustaining factual belief correction and reinforcing shared factual baselines in spite of cognitive decay and partisan resistance.

- **Rethinking Assumptions About Moral Content Selection: Moral-Emotional Content May Be Less Appealing Than Assumed**
  Published: 2026-08-21
  Authors: Isabella Rebasso; Yanna Krupnikov
  Affiliations: University of Michigan; University of Vienna
  DOI: 10.1080/10584609.2026.2715701
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2715701
  Abstract: Recent work has shown that moral-emotional content spreads rapidly on social media platforms such as X (Twitter). These patterns have led to concerns that social media facilitates the spread of moral outrage – potentially exacerbating political conflict and animosity. While most past work has focused on the supply side of this phenomenon, i.e. the transmission of morally charged political content, we consider the demand side: do people actually want to see this type of content in their information environment? In this paper, we first theoretically contextualize the motivations to engage with or avoid moral-emotional content. Then, through three pre-registered studies conducted in the U.S. in 2022 and 2023, we test whether people actively choose information that includes moral-emotional elements. Contrary to our initial expectations, we find that participants do not actively select moral-emotional content compared to neutral or emotional content. Despite previous findings on transmission, our results suggest that people may not want to see this type of content in their information environment. By focusing on selection rather than transmission, the findings presented in this paper add another nuance to an ongoing debate on the prevalence and consequences of online political outrage.

- **Assessing Civic Journalism Training: A Mixed-Method Applied Research Study**
  Published: 2026-08-18
  Authors: Joshua P. Darr; Susan Robinson
  Affiliations: Syracuse University; University of Wisconsin
  DOI: 10.1080/10584609.2026.2715698
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2715698
  Abstract: This mixed‑method applied research study examines how audiences are responding to news stories that are shaped by retraining efforts to help American journalists rebuild audiences. We wanted to know: were these trainings working? Do audiences choose stories that were influenced by these trainings over more traditional elite-focused, problem-oriented, conflict-driven stories? Working with three newsroom-training organizations, we report the results of a series of conjoint experiments that test how well stories abiding by new journalistic techniques of engagement, solutions and “good-conflict” framing play with audiences. We then use the findings from seven focus groups (n = 49) with surveys to explain why people may have responded the way they did in the tests. We found that audiences overwhelmingly selected and appreciated the re-framed content and reported that it makes them trust the news brands more. The focus group participants claimed such content would entice them to become members, subscribe, or even donate to the mainstream brand – if they came across it. However, only a few of the participants said they had heard about the news outlet. Though the principles behind these trainings may seem counterintuitive to some journalists, they offer a potential path forward in uncertain times for local news.

- **Captured participatory institutions: Theory and evidence from Venezuela's Consejos Comunales**
  Published: 2026-08-18
  Authors: Jared Abbott; Jose Morales‐Arilla
  Affiliations: Department of Political Science California State University‐Los Angeles Los Angeles California USA; Escuela de Gobierno y Transformación Pública Tecnologico de Monterrey Ciudad de México Mexico
  DOI: 10.1111/ajps.70077
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.70077
  Abstract: Abstract Can participatory institutions (PIs) facilitate development when captured by the government? We propose an original theoretical framework to investigate how changing incumbent priorities can shape the developmental and political effects of captured PIs. While patrons concerned with survival can force PIs to specialize in political activities, those pursuing regime consolidation may incentivize PIs to multitask, delivering developmental gains and political rewards. We deploy our framework to study Venezuela's Communal Councils.   Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502261470088
  Abstract: By analyzing 164 articles from communication journals published between 2002 and 2024, this scoping review systematically investigates temporal trends, conceptualizations, and operationalizations of transparency research within communication studies. The analysis identifies three primary conceptual dimensions of transparency: (1) information, encompassing both visibility and interpretation; (2) relations, functioning as an intermediary between dialogue and power; (3) values and norms, reflecting the tensions between ethical ideals and institutional constraints. Subsequent redistribution may involve professional brokers and traffickers. Compared with the drug trade, the illegal gun market is smaller and more exposed to law enforcement. While preventing dangerous people from obtaining guns is intrinsically difficult in a regime designed for broad accessibility to most adults, regulations can substantially affect illegal commerce, and some regulations reduce criminal use.

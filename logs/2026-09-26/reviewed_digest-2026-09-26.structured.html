Breadwinner backlash: The gendered effects of industrial decline
2026-09-22New This WeekDOI 10.1111/ajps.70080

Richard Clark; Roza Khoban; Noah Zucker

Assistant Professor, Department of Politics Princeton University Princeton New Jersey USA; Associate Professor, Department of Political Science University of Notre Dame Notre Dame Indiana USA; Postdoctoral Researcher, Department of Economics University of Zurich Zürich Switzerland

Abstract Low‐skilled men have long been vulnerable to globalization, decarbonization, and other forms of economic disruption. When shocks are realized, women often increase their paid labor to compensate for men's loss of income. We study how layoffs of low‐skilled men and concurrent shifts in workforce composition toward women affect voting. We theorize that this labor market rebalancing produces “nostalgic” coalitions of men and women that seek a return to male‐breadwinner family models. Such attitudes fuel right‐wing movements that pledge to protect conservative gender roles. Evidence from local labor markets and voting in the United States over the last two decades, as well as a longitudinal study tracking individual Americans over several decades, support this theory. These findings contribute a new gender‐based account of the globalization backlash.

American Political Science Review

Structural Injustice as Emergent Disorder
2026-09-25New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426101877

HOCHAN KIM

Bates College

Abstract Structural injustice remains a puzzling concept despite its increasing prominence. I distill recent theoretical challenges into a dilemma: either structural injustice lacks the normative credentials of injustice given that wrongdoing is inessential, or it is redundant given existing theories that trace injustice to culpable state and other institutional failures. I then develop a novel account of structural injustice, the Complexity View, that unravels this dilemma. The emergent properties of human actions in a complex society explain what is “structural” about structural injustice, specifically how it can arise without (culpable) wrongdoing. Structural injustice is unjust because it is wrong to contribute to social processes that cause harm and to passively accept preventable forms of vulnerability and deprivation as tragic misfortunes. But this account of structural injustice has a sobering implication: while complexity explains what distinguishes structural injustice from other wrongs, it also sets significant epistemic limits on social reform.

The Punitive Dimension of Presidential Particularism
2026-09-24New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426102020

LUKAS K. ALEXANDER

Washington University in St. Louis

Existing accounts of presidential particularism emphasize the targeted distribution of benefits to electorally valuable constituencies. This research note demonstrates that presidential particularism can also operate punitively, through the selective imposition of costs on constituencies that provided weaker electoral support for the president. I examine this dynamic through the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), a highly centralized and politicized agency created at the start of Donald Trump’s second term. Leveraging data on nearly 700,000 federal contracts linked to DOGE contract cuts and 2024 county-level election returns, I show that these cuts were systematically concentrated in counties that provided weaker electoral support for Trump. These patterns hold across contract- and county-level analyses. Together, these findings broaden theories of presidential particularism by showing that presidents may use administrative authority to impose costs on electorally oppositional constituencies. More broadly, I demonstrate how presidents can wield administrative power as a tool of partisan punishment.

Imperial Aid and Tax Mobilization in Late Colonial Africa
2026-09-21New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426101919

DIDAC QUERALT

Yale University

In 1929, the British Empire launched a four-decade development initiative to modernize colonial economies without self-government. Known as the Colonial Development and Welfare Fund, the program required colonial administrations to raise local taxes to cofinance development projects. This article shows that imperial aid expanded colonial taxation only after organizational reforms in the Colonial Service screened in officials who shared the metropole’s developmental agenda and made tax mobilization incentive-compatible with career advancement. Drawing on original data from 12 African colonies and extensive archival evidence, I show that aid increased tax revenues when patronage-based governors were replaced by career officers and promotion within the Colonial Service became conditional on fiscal performance. These findings highlight how bureaucratic incentives shaped the effectiveness of imperial aid and offer a new explanation for the expansion of fiscal capacity in late colonial Africa.

Democratic Media Performance and Democratization: A Global Longitudinal Perspective
2026-09-24New This WeekDOI 10.1177/00936502261489667

Florian Arendt

University of Vienna, Austria

Media that effectively perform their democratic functions are widely considered a key driver of democratization, yet longitudinal evidence on their impact remains limited. This study analyzed global annual time-series data on democratic media performance and democratization from 1946 to 2018. ARIMA models indicated a covariation between annual increases in democratic media performance and gains in democratization worldwide, measured by Vanhanen’s Index of Democratization. The analysis also revealed covariation with V-Dem’s liberal, participatory, deliberative, and egalitarian component indices of democratic quality. Evidence on temporal ordering, consistent with Granger causality, was found for Vanhanen’s index and the participatory and deliberative V-Dem component indices; evidence was marginal for the liberal component and absent for the egalitarian component. Acknowledging limitations regarding causal interpretation, the results are consistent with the claim that media can act as a leading force in democratic development rather than merely reflecting it, particularly in electoral, participatory, and deliberative dimensions.

Journal of Communication

Rejection of cross-cutting news depends on content, sources, and cultural contexts: evidence from online trace data
2026-09-22New This WeekDOI 10.1093/joc/jqag014

Wonjeong Jo; Magdalena Wojcieszak

Center for Excellence in Social Science, University of Warsaw , Warsaw,; Department of Communication, University of California , Davis, CA,

Abstract We examine the conditions in which people reject cross-cutting news in naturalistic settings of online comments. Theoretically, we account for article content and source to test whether commenters’ rejection and its intensity depend on whether cross-cutting news (a) is hostile to their in-group or favorable to the out-group and (b) is “unexpected” (from in-group sources) or “expected” (from out-group sources). Methodologically, we use online traces from news aggregators in South Korea (SK; 7,607 comments) and the United States (116,308 comments). We find that most commenters (79% in SK and 64% in the United States) reject cross-cutting news, with left-leaning commenters expressing stronger rejection than right-leaning commenters. Regarding article content, the SK commenters more strongly rejected in-group hostility, while the U.S. commenters rejected out-group favoritism more. Regarding article source, SK commenters more strongly rejected unexpected cross-cutting news from in-group sources than expected cross-cutting news from out-group sources. We discuss the implications of these findings.

Face-off: facial expression disclosure affects the processing of proxemic norms in virtual reality
2026-09-23New This WeekDOI 10.1093/jcmc/zmag021

Kun Xu; Mengsu Shi; Jiayue Li; Zhe Zhou; Yunxiao Chen; Sylvia Chan-Olmsted

College of Communication, Boston University , Boston, MA,; College of Journalism and Communications, University of Florida , Gainesville, FL,; School of Journalism and Communication, Tsinghua University , Beijing,

Abstract As virtual reality (VR) technologies support various forms of social interactions, questions have emerged regarding whether and how interpersonal proxemic norms are enacted in VR. Meanwhile, VR’s body tracking features, particularly face and eye tracking, have fostered more natural and vivid communication. However, these tracking features also intensify users’ pressure to manage self-image and privacy. Drawing on lab experiments, psychophysiological measures, and qualitative interpretations, this study seeks to understand how face tracking influences individuals’ cognitive and affective reactions to proxemic norm violation in VR. The findings suggest that although participants perceive proxemic norm violations as improper, physiologically arousing, and unpleasant, emerging norms in VR have subtly shifted these reactions. Participants’ gender and facial expression visibility separately or jointly moderate the effects of norm violation on perceived politeness, identifiability, and social presence. The potential theoretical integration of presence, transformed social interaction, and objective self-awareness is discussed to inform future research.

Nature

Banking records reveal characteristics of financial abuse
2026-09-23New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41586-026-11049-7

Anna Trendl; Nicola Sharp-Jeffs; Karen Perrier; Jane Rodrick; John Gathergood; David Leake

Abstract The experience of financial abuse, a form of domestic abuse involving control over money and finances, can be severe and long-lasting 1–3 , yet difficult to document 4 . Evidence on the everyday experiences of victim-survivors in the context of financial abuse is drawn primarily from small-scale, qualitative surveys 5,6 . Using anonymized banking data from a major UK retail bank, we compared the financial outcomes of a group of female victim-survivors of financial abuse ( n = 5,428; identified from customer disclosures to the bank) with a control group ( n = 15,602; consisting of individuals with no known disclosures). The control group matched the demographic and socioeconomic characteristics of the victim-survivor group 7 years before disclosure. A comprehensive set of 373 transactional and non-transactional financial outcomes were analysed over this period. Results indicate that, compared with controls, victim-survivors exhibited depleting savings and increasing debts, culminating in missed payments and declining credit scores. They also spent less on self-care, incurred higher transport and legal costs, increased cash withdrawals, changed addresses and passwords more frequently and were more likely to claim welfare benefits. Our results demonstrate how victim-survivors lose financial independence, experience economic instability and suffer financial distress.

Disentangling interaction and bias effects in opinion dynamics of large language models
2026-09-22New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-77340-3

Vincent C. Brockers; David A. Ehrlich; Viola Priesemann

Abstract Large Language Models are increasingly used to simulate human opinion dynamics, yet the effect of genuine interaction is often obscured by systematic biases. We develop a Bayesian framework to disentangle and quantify three such biases: (i) A topic bias toward the LLM’s default stance; (ii) an agreement bias favoring agreement to the prompted statement irrespective of the question; and (iii) an anchoring bias toward the initiating agent’s stance. We apply this framework to various LLMs that performed multi-step dialogs on 12 different questions from climate change and societal justice to music preferences. We find that opinion trajectories tend to quickly converge to a shared attractor, with the influence of both interaction and biases decaying over time, and with the impact of biases differing between LLMs. In addition, we show that fine-tuning an LLM on different sets of strongly opinionated statements (including misinformation) shifts the opinion attractor correspondingly. By exposing stark differences between LLMs and providing quantitative tools for comparing interaction and bias contributions to opinion shifts in LLM agent discussions, our approach highlights both promises and pitfalls of using LLMs as proxies for human behavior.

Mortality and healthcare services in Pennsylvania state prisons following extreme ambient temperatures
2026-09-22New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-78012-y

Jaquelyn L. Jahn; Claudia N. Anderson; Jessica T. Simes; Leah H. Schinasi; Michael Leung; Josiah L. Kephart; Bruce Western

Extreme temperatures pose severe risks for population health and healthcare systems. However, little research has examined implications for incarcerated populations, who may be particularly vulnerable given their limited control over their thermal environments. Here, we show that among people imprisoned in Pennsylvania (2000-2023), both warmer and cooler temperatures were significantly associated with higher rates of mortality, transfers to outside medical facilities and prison infirmary admissions, relative to outcome-specific reference minimum threshold temperatures. As temperature extremes become more frequent and severe, policy action including decarceration and the provision of accessible prison healthcare is urgently needed to protect the health of incarcerated populations. Extreme hot and cold temperatures are determinants of mortality for incarcerated populations and this manuscript shows that temperature extremes increase demand on already strained prison healthcare systems.

Post-2020 conservation priorities are threatened by global mining
2026-09-21New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-77626-6

Kaixuan Pan; Niels Raes; Wassim Le Lann; Leon Marshall; Ana Feijoo Quezada; Valerio Barbarossa; Merijn Moens; Sarah Anderson; Rene Kleijn; Jacobus C. Biesmeijer

Abstract The United Nations has committed to halting and reversing biodiversity loss by 2030. Despite this commitment, mining remains a major driver of biodiversity decline, particularly in conservation priority zones (CPZs) and areas with high species extinction risk. Regardless of growing awareness, the full extent of current and future mining threats to biodiversity remains inadequately quantified, limiting effective global conservation strategies. Here, we quantify the threats of 145,000 km² of current mining land to biodiversity. We find that 34% (49,000 km²) of these mining areas overlap with CPZs, intersecting the ranges of more than 2,300 species, including many classified as threatened. These threats span 14 biomes, with disproportionately high relative impacts in fragile ecosystems such as Mangroves and the Mediterranean. Furthermore, an additional 43% (12 million km²), 29% (2 million km²), and 37% (4 million km²) of land potentially exploitable for metals, non-metals, and coal, respectively, overlap with CPZs, highlighting the heightened risks from future mining expansion without consideration for biodiversity. Our findings offer actionable insights for policymakers and industry stakeholders, guiding conservation efforts to mitigate biodiversity loss and align mining practices with global conservation goals, including the UN Sustainable Development Goals and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework.

Political Actors and Their Media Practices: Analyzing the Role of Media Participation in Democratic Backsliding and Resilience
2026-09-22New This WeekDOI 10.1080/10584609.2026.2735919

Daniel Kreiss; Kate Wright

University of Edinburgh; University of North Carolina

This paper draws together insights from political science and political communication to advance a new understanding of the role of media in democratic backsliding and resilience. The central argument is that all political actors may also be media actors, although they may do so anonymously or use false identities. Thus, mass participation in media enables mass participation in political change, with better and worse democratic outcomes. As such, when analyzing backsliding and resilience scholars need to move away from focusing solely on particular institutions and actors with presumed democratic functions, as well as assuming that media and expressive freedom will necessarily serve democratic ends. Instead, we advocate analyzing how the participatory media practices of various actors interact with one another via communicative structures within processes of backsliding and resilience. Participatory media practices that prevent democratic breakdown include disseminating early warnings of backsliding, exposing executive errors and corruption, coordinating pro-democratic mobilization, and platforming the opposition, as well as countering disinformation and delegitimizing democratic institutions and processes. We also indicate how participatory media practices may aid democratic bounceback, as well as the challenges involved in reimagining the media infrastructure needed to sustain democratic recovery in the long-term. Thus, we argue that democratic backsliding and resilience should be reconceptualized as profoundly participatory, mediated processes.

Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences

Dynamic attribute activation explains gaze biases in choice
2026-09-25New This WeekDOI 10.1073/pnas.2603674123

Xiaozhi Taro Yang; Sudeep Bhatia

Department of Psychology, University of Pennsylvania; School of Arts and sciences Mind Center for Outreach, Research, and Education, University of Pennsylvania

People tend to choose the options they look at more. Prior work explains this effect by assuming that visual attention increases preference for the attended option. However, it remains unclear why looking at an option increases its preference and when this relationship is weakened. Using an experimental paradigm that combines eye-tracking and a think-aloud protocol, we provide evidence that visual attention shapes preference by activating attributes associated with the attended option. Critically, the content of these activated attributes depends on contextual factors such as stimulus desirability and behavioral goals. In standard choice settings, where the goal is to choose the preferred option, visual attention primarily activates positive attributes. In contrast, when these contextual factors are incongruent, activated attributes become less positive, reducing the influence of visual attention on choice. To account for these patterns, we propose a computational model of gaze-driven attribute activation. Our model subsumes existing theories as special cases, and uniquely predicts which attributes are brought to mind by gaze and how they shape downstream decisions. Together, our work offers a mechanistic explanation of a fundamental driver of choice as well as its boundary conditions.

AICO: Feature significance tests for supervised learning
2026-09-23New This WeekDOI 10.1073/pnas.2530045123

Kay Giesecke; Enguerrand Horel; Chartsiri Jirachotkulthorn

Hasso Plattner Institute, University of Potsdam; Institute for Computational and Mathematical Engineering, Stanford University; Upstart Inc.

Machine learning is central to modern science, industry, and policy, yet its predictive power often comes at the cost of transparency: We rarely know which input features drive a model’s predictions. Without such understanding, researchers cannot draw reliable conclusions, practitioners cannot ensure fairness or accountability, and policymakers cannot trust or govern model-based decisions. Existing tools for assessing feature influence are limited; most lack statistical guarantees, and many require costly retraining or surrogate modeling, making them impractical for large modern models. We introduce AICO (Add-In COvariates), a broadly applicable framework that turns model interpretability into an efficient statistical exercise. AICO tests whether each feature contributes to predictive performance by masking its information and measuring the resulting change. The method provides exact, finite-sample feature P -values and CIs for feature importance through a simple, nonasymptotic hypothesis testing procedure. It requires no retraining, surrogate modeling, or distributional assumptions, making it feasible for large-scale algorithms. In both controlled experiments and real applications, from credit scoring to mortgage-behavior prediction, AICO identifies variables that contribute to model behavior, providing a scalable and statistically principled path toward transparent and trustworthy machine learning.

Science still has expertise; what it’s lost is authority
2026-09-24New This WeekDOI 10.1126/science.aem5190

H. Holden Thorp

H. Holden Thorp is Editor-in-Chief of the Science journals.

In the years since the pandemic and the political turmoil surrounding the American scientific establishment, much time has been spent discussing why science has lost influence on public policy in the United States. A time when scientific advice about vaccination, nutrition, and environmental regulation was more readily heeded seems a distant memory. Scientific expertise has been decoupled from a sense of authority and standing to dictate policy on matters ranging from public health to climate change. The explanations for this separation include the rise of technologies like the internet and artificial intelligence, mistakes made by scientists and scientific institutions, and weaponization of anti-science ideas by political actors. But what if there’s a larger historical trend that suggests the disconnect between expertise and authority was inevitable? A new idea in philosophy called the “unicontext” suggests that the advance of technology, along with basic human desires, has been leading to this point for the past century.

Quantifying CAHOOTS: Measuring a mobile crisis response program’s impact on police response through call diversion and prevention
2026-09-25New This WeekDOI 10.1126/sciadv.aeb8064

Nathan Burton; Claire Herbert; Rori Rohlfs

Department of Data Science, University of Oregon, Eugene, OR 97403, USA.; Department of Sociology, University of Oregon, Eugene, OR 97403, USA.; Institute for Ecology and Evolution, University of Oregon, Eugene, OR 97403, USA.

Mobile Crisis Response Programs (MCRPs) have emerged as alternatives to police for emergency mental and behavioral health calls, yet their effectiveness in diverting calls from police remains contested. CAHOOTS (Crisis Assistance Helping Out On The Streets), the United States’ longest-running MCRP, has been subject to widely varying ad hoc diversion rate estimates, from 3 to 20%. We use a systematic difference-in-differences design to estimate the causal effects of CAHOOTS on police call volume by introducing three estimators. The Substitution Diversion Rate (SDR) quantifies MCRP responses that directly replace police responses and estimates the rate at 18% for CAHOOTS. Our results indicate that in addition to substituting for police response, CAHOOTS was associated with reductions in police-handled call volume consistent with a prevention effect. The Prevention-adjusted Diversion Rate (PDR) accounts for both call substitution and prevention, and we estimate it at 23% for CAHOOTS. Last, the Overlapping-mandate Diversion Rate (ODR) quantifies the proportion of mutually answerable calls handled by an MCRP. We estimate CAHOOTS’ ODR at 50%. Our findings provide evidence that MCRPs significantly reduce police response call volume through direct substitution and likely incident prevention.

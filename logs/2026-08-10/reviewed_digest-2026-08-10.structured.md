- **The Knowledge Gap in a High-Choice Media Environment: Experimental Evidence from Online Search**
  Published: 2026-08-06
  Authors: Roberto Ulloa; Leonard Tiedemann; Peter Selb; Celina Kacperski
  Affiliations: Humboldt University of Berlin, Germany; Seeburg Castle University, Austria; University of Konstanz
  DOI: 10.1080/10584609.2026.2708131
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2708131
  Abstract: Persistent inequalities in political knowledge are a central concern in political communication. We organize the mechanisms underlying the knowledge-gap literature by distinguishing between individual preconditions, structural features of the information environment, and topic characteristics. Within this framework, we note that self-directed information seeking, a prototypical form of intentional exposure, has received little attention despite its importance in navigating today's complex information environment. We conducted a field experiment in Germany combining randomized encouragements and passive browser tracking to examine how individuals with varying education levels acquire policy-specific knowledge through online search. Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions (verbal encouragement, financial encouragement, or control) to seek information on three salient policy topics differing in divisiveness and complexity (child support, energy transition, and cannabis legalization). We estimate both intention-to-treat (ITT) and local average treatment effects (LATE) of information seeking on post-search knowledge outcomes, with a focus on education and civic knowledge as moderators. While the interventions equalized information-seeking behavior, the results provide some support for the knowledge gap hypothesis: knowledge gains were concentrated among participants with higher education or baseline civic knowledge, who, according to our post-hoc exploratory analyses, appeared more effective at navigating search results. These findings indicate that a narrowing of knowledge inequalities goes beyond motivation: it calls for both individual-level interventions to strengthen citizens'skills and structural-level adaptations to foster more equitable learning environments.

- **Twitter Conversations: Gendered Issue Publics Among Women**
  Published: 2026-08-03
  Authors: Caley Hewitt; Ayla Oden; Nichole M. Bauer
  Affiliations: Louisiana State University, Baton Rouge, USA
  DOI: 10.1177/00936502261470086
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502261470086
  Abstract: We investigate how gender shapes women’s political discussion on Twitter using an issue publics framework. We test whether the conditions for gendered issue publics to form among women around feminine issues exist using data on political discussion via social media in the 2020 and 2022 elections. Our data includes political discussion among political candidates, elites, and non-elites, or regular people. Our results reveal several key findings. First, we find that men elites were more likely to discuss feminine issues, such as health care, compared to women elites, but we found no gender differences between regular Twitter users in our sample. Finally, we show that in 2022, the Dobbs decision that rescinded the constitutional right to an abortion in the U.S. increased the salience of this issue among both women and men. Overall, our research suggests that the conditions for issue publics among women may not strongly exist as we find that non-elite men talk about feminine issues too.

- **Let Them Eat Pie: Addressing Sample Selection in Multiparty Elections**
  Published: 2026-08-05
  Authors: Ali Kagalwala; Thiago M. Q. Moreira; Guy D. Whitten; Yongzhi Xu
  Affiliations: Louisiana State University; Syracuse University; Texas A&M University; University of International Business and Economics
  DOI: 10.1017/pan.2026.10049
  Link: https://doi.org/10.1017/pan.2026.10049
  Abstract: Abstract Elections are central to the study of politics. When studying parties’ vote shares across districts, scholars are encouraged to use compositional-outcome models in order to test their theories about what factors shape the dynamics of electoral support. Existing compositional modeling approaches incorrectly deal with scenarios in which not all parties compete in every electoral district. Because unobserved factors that affect a party’s decisions to contest a district are likely correlated with its performance in districts where the party fielded candidates, failing to account for partial contestation is likely to result in sample selection bias. We illustrate the utility of our approach by analyzing the 2017 and 2019 U.K. parliamentary elections in English constituencies.

  Abstract: Abstract Inferring hidden environmental structures, which commonly involves learning arbitrary probability distributions from limited samples, is essential to optimal and adaptive behaviors across various cognitive domains.   Abstract: Despite concern about exposure to content from untrustworthy sources on social media, little is known about the frequency or effects of exposure to their content. We examine 2020 data from all active US adults on Facebook and Instagram to measure exposure to content from Pages, groups, and web domains on Facebook and public accounts on Instagram that repeatedly publish misinformation. We find that average users saw relatively little content in their feeds from these untrustworthy sources; exposure was highly concentrated.   Abstract: Newly digitized mortgage records from 38 US states reveal the profound racial inequality of two mortgage programmes during the 1930s and 1940s. These loans, supported by the Federal Housing Administration and the Veterans Administration, were mainly responsible for the emergence of a US society that idealized home ownership. However, Black households were systematically excluded from receiving such loans. By contrast, immigrants received mortgages proportional to their number.   Abstract: Across many high-income societies, anxiety about demographic decline has become commonplace. Fertility rates have fallen below replacement level, populations are aging rapidly, and governments worry about labor shortages, pension sustainability, and the future of welfare systems. These concerns are legitimate. Yet the conventional narrative does not always capture another important consequence of declining birth rates: dejuvenation, the progressive reduction of the demographic, economic, cultural and political weight of younger generations in society. Although low fertility alone does not immediately transform institutions, the cumulative effect of decades of low births combined with rising longevity does. The result is a thinner presence of younger people in the labor market, in entrepreneurship and innovation, and in the democratic debates in which long-term priorities are contested.

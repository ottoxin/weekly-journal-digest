Teaching the past, informing the present? School history education and attitudes toward foreign countries
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1111/ajps.70082

Soyoung Lee; Jeongmin Park

Assistant Professor, Department of Political Science Yale University New Haven Connecticut USA; Postdoctoral Prize Fellow, Department of Politics Oxford University Nuffield College Oxford UK

Abstract How does school history education emphasizing past grievances with other countries impact attitudes toward conflict and cooperation with the countries today? We examine this question using original data from 34 classrooms across 17 high schools in Seoul, South Korea. We first test whether exposure to anti‐Japanese content in school history education negatively impacts students' attitudes toward cooperation with Japan. We then compare how the attitudes of students educated with a more pro‐US textbook differ from those using a more anti‐US one. We find that school history education influenced what historical events students believed as “facts” and their evaluation of historical events. However, the effect on students' historical memories did not by itself automatically translate into corresponding attitudes toward present‐day Japan or the United States. Our findings offer various implications for studies on international rivalries and historical memories, as well as for debates on the political effects of school education.

American Political Science Review

The Idea of a Counter-Structure: Rethinking Collective Action against Climate Injustice
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426101920

JONATHAN MASIN-PETERS

Harvard University

This article argues that addressing the structural injustices of climate change requires rethinking collective action. Disruptive and prefigurative politics are often treated as separate collective action repertoires, but I argue that a just energy transition requires that they work in tandem. To capture this interaction, I propose the idea of “counter-structural” collective action, in which disruptive protest creates windows of opportunity that prefigurative alternatives can then fill. Through analysis of two UK climate movements, Transition Towns (TTs) and Camps for Climate Action (CCA), I show how activists reconfigure structural elements to build new, carbon-free social and political forms. The theory of counter-structural action provides a framework for assessing the empirical cases. The cases, in turn, illustrate a broader conception of collective action that better reflects the social theory grounding structural injustice. Together, the theory and cases support the dual aims of climate justice: challenging fossil-fuel path dependence and advancing egalitarianism.

When Can Clientelism Serve Democratic Ends? A Democratic Theory of Distributive Politics
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426101816

SAMUEL ELY BAGG; UDIT BHATIA

King’s College London; University of South Carolina

This article employs normative democratic theory to examine a counterintuitive suggestion raised repeatedly by empirical scholars of clientelism: namely, that practices of particularistic exchange might sometimes serve democratic ends. Certainly, most particularistic practices are widely agreed to undermine democracy, on both intrinsic and instrumental grounds. Yet some observers resist blanket condemnation—and in particular, many highlight the redeeming democratic virtues of certain practices that seem to facilitate collective organization among disempowered groups. Drawing on “realist” approaches to democratic theory, we vindicate this basic insight while clarifying its scope conditions. Countervailing power is a crucial democratic priority, and programmatic methods of promoting it sometimes face serious obstacles: as when clientelist practices are deeply entrenched, or incumbents capture the tools of programmatic competition. Under such conditions, we conclude, democracy-seeking leaders may justifiably employ particularistic practices to promote countervailing power—but only if they respect five constraints tethering them strictly to that goal.

Crime and Participation
2026-10-06New This WeekDOI 10.1017/s0003055426102007

DAVID DOHERTY; G. AGUSTIN MARKARIAN; MENA WHALEN; DANA GARBARSKI

Loyola University Chicago

Does exposure to crime mobilize or demobilize political participation? We report findings from a preregistered design, leveraging data from an original survey of 21 major U.S. cities and five waves of the Cooperative Election Study. We assess the relationships between direct victimization, local (ZCTA-level) crime rates, and perceived neighborhood safety and two outcomes: turnout (including validated turnout) and non-electoral participation. We find that direct victimization is significantly associated with more non-electoral engagement but lower turnout. The negative estimated effects of victimization on turnout exceed the magnitude of positive effects associated with common get-out-the-vote efforts. We find only limited evidence that crime rates are associated with participation. Perceived neighborhood safety is associated with higher rates of non-electoral, but not electoral, participation. Our findings offer new, comprehensive evidence on the relationship between crime exposure and political participation and illustrate the importance of distinguishing between types of exposure and forms of participation.

The sequential replicant effect in interpersonal sincerity: evidence from AI-mediated apology and gratitude
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1093/jcmc/zmag024

Bolin Cao; Zhenming Li

Cardiff Business School, Cardiff University , Cardiff, Wales,; School of Media and Communication, Shenzhen University , Shenzhen, Guangdong,

Abstract Sincerity, a core interpersonal value that mobilizes emotional resources, faces distinct challenges in AI-mediated interpersonal contexts. Extending the Replicant Effect from informational trust to interpersonal sincerity, this study examines how AIMC awareness and AI involvement cues shape sincerity judgments and subsequent relational responses. Drawing on two pre-registered, two-round studies in apology (N = 546) and gratitude (N = 561) scenarios, we found that AI involvement cues reliably reduced perceived sincerity. Primed AIMC awareness showed a more sequential influence on later no-cue evaluations through AIMC suspicion. High-ambiguity AI cues produced sequential effects only in the gratitude context. These findings suggest that hybrid human-AI authorship may reshape how interpersonal sincerity is inferred and validated, while also showing that the effects of AI cues and AIMC awareness depend on cue clarity and relational context.

Nature Communications

Distinguishing learning asymmetry from perseveration in behavioral models
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-78198-1

Juan Vidal-Perez; Raymond J. Dolan; Rani Moran

Abstract A central challenge in cognitive science is to distinguish between multiple processes that can result in similar behaviors. In reinforcement learning, one prominent example concerns two potential drivers of choice repetition: a learning asymmetry, in which agents learn differently from outcomes depending on their valence and/or whether they confirm choice, and choice perseveration, an outcome-independent tendency to repeat past choices. Evidence for asymmetric learning has typically relied on computational models that control for perseveration, or specific behavioral markers. Here, we show both these approaches have critical flaws and can spuriously detect learning asymmetries even in perseverative, symmetric-learning agents. To address this, we introduce a statistical test that distinguishes genuine learning asymmetries from spurious effects. Applying this test to a large dataset spanning ten published experiments across four studies, we find that some previously reported confirmation biases are fragile, albeit others remain robust even at an across-study level. Finally, we propose a task design that can yield a more valid qualitative signature of confirmation bias. Our approach provides a reliable framework for disentangling processes underlying choice repetition, while providing tools for the wider research community that can minimize potential spurious effects arising from process mimicry and biased parameter estimation.

Emergence of polarization in networks of large language model agents
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-78228-y

Jinghua Piao; Zhihong Lu; Chen Gao; Fengli Xu; Qinghua Hu; Fernando P. Santos; Yong Li; James Evans

Abstract Rapid advances in large language models (LLMs) have empowered autonomous agents to generate social networks, communicate, and form shared and diverging opinions on political issues. However, our understanding of their collective behaviours and underlying mechanisms remains incomplete. In this paper, we simulate networked systems involving thousands of LLM agents across different backbone models (GPT-3.5, GPT-4o, ChatGLM, Llama-3, and DeepSeek-V3), in which agents interact through LLM-guided conversations and update their opinions over time, resulting in the emergence of opinion polarization. We discover that these agents spontaneously develop their own social network with properties characteristic of human social networks, such as homophilic clustering. The collective opinions of these LLM agents evolve in ways that exhibit behavioural patterns consistent with social phenomena and mechanisms widely discussed in empirical studies of human behaviour and opinion-dynamics models. This consistency suggests that LLM agents can serve as a valuable synthetic testbed for exploring hypothetical intervention strategies in networked LLM-agent systems. Using this testbed, we further examine the effects of a range of network- and individual-level interventions, such as promoting more diverse interactions and reducing confirmation bias. Overall, this work not only sheds light on subtle opinion dynamics and collective behaviour of LLM agents from a network perspective, but also demonstrates their potential for testing hypotheses about opinion dynamics and intervention strategies.

The limits of Antarctic conservation governance
2026-10-06New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-77606-w

Zachary T. Carter; Larissa Lubiana Botelho; Steven L. Chown; Parsa Yaryab; Nadiah P. Kristensen; Kerrie A. Wilson; Daniela Liggett; Michael Bode

Abstract The Antarctic region exerts a profound influence on the Earth system, making its conservation fundamental to sustaining planetary health and human wellbeing. The Antarctic Treaty System (ATS) holds near-exclusive authority for environmental protection across the region, yet questions remain regarding its capacity to address a growing range of conservation threats. We investigate this by analysing over 90,000 scientific and policy documents (1961–2023) to examine how conservation threats are discussed across science and ATS policy deliberations. We find that threats originating more locally show clearer evidence of progression through the ATS’s decision-making system. Threats originating within the Antarctic Treaty Area show repeated progression towards governance action, whereas external threats, including climate change, remain under prolonged deliberation or receive limited engagement altogether. These patterns suggest that the ATS has demonstrated capacity to translate sustained engagement into governance action for threats within its remit, while comparable action remains less evident for threats originating beyond it. This distinction is increasingly consequential because some of Antarctica’s greatest threats originate outside the region. Strengthening coordination between the ATS and broader global governance frameworks may therefore be critical to addressing them.

Narrowing urban heat disparities alongside faster greening in low-income neighborhoods across cities in the United States
2026-10-05New This WeekDOI 10.1038/s41467-026-78468-y

Jie Cao; Weiqi Zhou; Timon McPhearson; Jing Wang; Haoran Hou; Jing Liu

Urban heat disproportionately burdens socioeconomically vulnerable communities, yet how these disparities change over time remains poorly understood. Here we show that heat disparities between low- and high-income neighborhoods generally narrowed across 55 major cities in the United States from 1986 to 2020, although substantial inequities persisted. This narrowing coincided with faster environmental improvements in disadvantaged neighborhoods, particularly greater increases in greenness. Disparity trajectories varied across climate zones, with temperate and cold cities showing narrowing heat gaps, whereas arid cities more often showed persistent or widening gaps. At the city level, environmental disparities followed diverse pathways depending on changes in both overall environmental conditions and the distribution of change between income groups. Changes in greenness disparities were more strongly associated with changes in heat disparities than were changes in cooling efficiency. These findings highlight the dynamic nature of urban environmental inequality and the importance of equitable greening in urban climate adaptation. The Politics of the “Non-Political”: A Conceptual Review
2026-10-08New This WeekDOI 10.1080/10584609.2026.2745844

Yufan Guo; Yibin Fan

The Chinese University of Hong Kong; University of Washington

Recent literature has documented a growing entanglement between political processes and many nonpolitical entities. This calls into question the traditional presupposition of politics as a self-contained sphere, highlighting the need to clarify how the conceptual relationship between the political and the nonpolitical has been established. In line with a relational understanding of politics, we provide a boundary analysis by looking at the “non-political” labeling in 140 empirical studies published in political science and communication journals. Two major conceptual treatments are identified: categorical, treating the “non-political” as an inherent, exclusive feature; and contextual, viewing it as a temporary, transformative state. Intellectual traditions, theoretical orientations, methodological standards, and rhetorical norms in academic knowledge production may explain why this boundary has been constructed in such different ways. We call for a more active reconciliation between these treatments, and argue that the nonpolitical, as well as (de)politicization, should be recognized as a meaningful analytical lens. It signifies a relational stance against the explicit, institutionalized politics in political communication studies, offering an indirect yet heuristically productive response to the question of what the political is.

Does the TikTok Feed Lean Right? Exposure to Political Content Among Non-Partisan Users in Germany
2026-10-05New This WeekDOI 10.1080/10584609.2026.2733320

Jasper Tjaden; Johannes Wolfgram; Aaron Philipp; Sarah Weissmann; Licia Bobzien; Ulrich Kohler; Roland Verwiebe

University of Potsdam

We evaluated disparities in exposure to partisan content among new, nonpartisan TikTok users. We performed a sock puppet audit using 118 automated users in Germany ahead of three regional elections in 2024 and the federal election in 2025 (N = 266.161 videos). The design allowed us to account for past individual user behavior, user networks, location, and time. Overall, general political content constitutes 5%–7% of nonpolitical user feeds in our sample. When nonpartisan users encounter unsolicited political content, it is more likely to feature videos mentioning and supporting the far-right populist Alternative für Deutschland (AfD) party relative to all other parties. Further descriptive analysis shows that content by the AfD is associated with a higher volume of accounts and a higher number of viral videos.

The more you automate, the less you see: Hidden pitfalls of autonomous AI scientists
2026-10-05New This WeekDOI 10.1073/pnas.2610214123

Ziming Luo; Atoosa Kasirzadeh; Nihar B. Shah

Computer Science Department, School of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University; Department of Philosophy, Dietrich College of Humanities and Social Sciences, Carnegie Mellon University; Machine Learning Department, School of Computer Science, Carnegie Mellon University

The rapid rise of autonomous AI scientists marks a paradigm shift in scientific discovery by automating the research lifecycle. Yet their rushed development has outpaced critical oversight, leaving key workflow decisions dangerously unscrutinized. We present a much-needed systematic analysis of open-source AI scientist systems, investigating four primary pitfalls: inappropriate benchmark selection, data leakage, metric misuse, and post hoc selection bias. Through controlled experiments that isolate each pitfall, we find systematic vulnerabilities across two representative open-source systems. Crucially, we find that these flaws are largely invisible at the level of the final manuscript, suggesting that current manuscript-centric peer review paradigms are fundamentally insufficient for ensuring the integrity of automated research. We further propose mitigation strategies and demonstrate that access to full workflow artifacts (log traces and code) enables more effective auditing. Our findings suggest that journals, conferences, and researchers should move beyond manuscript-only evaluation toward process auditing the end-to-end workflow artifacts of AI scientist systems.

Science Advances

Costly missed connections: Visitation and misconduct in California prisons
2026-10-09New This WeekDOI 10.1126/sciadv.aed8424

Yilin Zhuo; Kristin Turney; Naomi F. Sugie; M. Keith Chen; Emily Owens

Anderson School of Management, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles, CA 90095, USA.; Balbec Capital LP, New York, NY 10016, USA.; Department of Criminology, Law and Society, University of California, Irvine, Irvine, CA 92697, USA.; Department of Economics, University of California, Irvine, Irvine, CA 92697, USA.; Department of Sociology, University of California, Irvine, Irvine, CA 92697, USA.; Department of Sociology, University of California, Los Angeles, Los Angeles, CA 90095, USA.

Social isolation is a major public health crisis with particularly deleterious consequences for vulnerable populations including incarcerated people. Theoretically, visitation from family should reduce social isolation and improve outcomes among incarcerated people, but existing quasi-experimental research provides limited support for the relationship between visitation and in-custody outcomes. We revisit the effect of visitation on in-custody misconduct—an important but administratively defined measure of institutional safety—by exploiting the fact that fluctuations in gasoline prices create unanticipated shifts in visitation uncorrelated with other factors associated with misconduct. We combine this quasi-experimental variation with smartphone mobility data identifying prison visits from cell phone pings and administrative data on misconduct in California prisons. We find that increases in visits induced by changes in transportation costs are negatively related to misconduct (particularly use-of-force events and personal battery), with effects concentrated immediately after visitation. Although the policy implications are subtle, these findings suggest that some strategies that reduce visitation barriers could be cost-effective ways to reduce prison violence. More broadly, they suggest that social connections have causal repercussions for violence and safety in extreme institutional environments.

Mesoscale structures in signed networks
2026-10-09New This WeekDOI 10.1126/sciadv.aee5886

Wei Zhang; Olga Boichak; Tristram J. Alexander; Tiago P. Peixoto; Eduardo G. Altmann

Center for Critical Computational Studies, Goethe University Frankfurt, 60629 Frankfurt am Main, Germany.; Centre for Complex Systems, University of Sydney, Sydney, 2006 NSW, Australia.; Complexity Science Hub, 1030 Vienna, Austria.; Department of Network and Data Science, Central European University, 1100 Vienna, Austria.; IT:U Interdisciplinary Transformation University, 4040 Linz, Austria.; Institute of Computer Science, Goethe University Frankfurt, 60629 Frankfurt am Main, Germany.; School of Art, Communication, and English, University of Sydney, Sydney, 2006 NSW, Australia.; School of Mathematics and Statistics, University of Sydney, Sydney, 2006 NSW, Australia.; School of Physics, University of Sydney, Sydney, 2006 NSW, Australia.

Mesoscale structures in signed networks have been studied under the limiting assumption of the validity of social balance theory, which predicts positive connections within groups and negative connections between groups. Here, we propose and apply a methodology that overcomes this limitation and is able to find and characterize also the different possible unbalanced structures in signed networks. Applying our methodology to 24 empirical networks, from social-political, financial, and biological domains, we find that unbalanced mesoscale structures are prevalent in real-world networks, including cases with substantial balance at the microscale of triangles. In particular, we find that assortativity often prevails regardless of the interaction sign and that core-periphery structures are typical in online social networks. Our findings highlight the complexity of mesoscale relational structures, the importance of using computational methods that are a priori agnostic to specific patterns, and the importance of independently evaluating micro- and mesoscale predictions of social balance theory.

Biological aging clocks offer estimations of aging and dementia, yet scalability is limited. We introduce a large-scale, cross-national speech clock derived from 2928 individuals across five Latin American countries, spanning healthy controls (HCs), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and non-language/language-dominant frontotemporal dementia (nldFTD/ldFTD). Multimodal acoustic and linguistic features were trained with supervised models to estimate chronological age, generating speech age gaps (SAGs) as cross-sectional markers of deviations from chronological age, with positive values interpreted as relatively older-appearing speech profiles.
